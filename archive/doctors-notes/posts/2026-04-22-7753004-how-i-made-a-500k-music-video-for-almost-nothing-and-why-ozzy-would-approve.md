## The Workflow Nobody Is Talking About

Here's exactly how I built a nearly six-minute cinematic music video as a one-man operation:

**Step 1 — Gemini for prompt engineering.** I used [Google Gemini](https://gemini.google.com/) to generate the visual prompts. This is not a small thing. Getting AI image and video tools to produce something coherent, let alone *cinematic*, requires knowing how to speak the language. Gemini helped me build that vocabulary at scale — describing the Giger aesthetic (dark biomechanical surrealism, if you need a reference point — H.R. Giger was the Swiss artist behind the visual design of *Alien*, and his work looks like a nightmare that a machine had about being alive), the color palette, the texture, the feel.

**Step 2 — Kling AI and Google Flow for imagery.** The bulk of the still and motion visuals came from these two tools. Here's the important distinction: [Kling AI](https://kling.ai/) did a significantly better job rendering the band members with recognizable likeness. If you need humans who actually look like specific humans, Kling is where you go. [Google Flow](https://labs.google/flow/about) handled a lot of the environmental and atmospheric work.

**Step 3 — RunwayML for lip sync.** The sections where you see me singing — that's [RunwayML](https://runwayml.com/). It's not perfect, and generation is a snail's pace, but it's close enough to work inside a hellscape aesthetic, which, frankly, is a feature not a bug.

**Step 4 — VEGAS Pro to stitch it all together.** Every clip, every transition, every sync point — assembled in [VEGAS Pro](https://vfx.borisfx.com/vegas-pro-free-trial) into a cohesive narrative arc. Nearly six minutes of content that holds together visually because I treated it like an editor, not just a content creator.

The total? A fraction of professional production costs. Not "cheaper." *Radically* cheaper.

## Here's Where Everyone Gets It Wrong

The loudest argument against AI in creative work goes something like this: *"AI is going to put artists and production companies out of work."*

I've heard it from musicians. I've heard it from graphic designers. I've heard it in comment sections from people who've never hired a production company in their lives.

And here's what I want to say to all of them, backed by actual economics and not just vibes:

**I was never going to be anyone's client.**

A top-tier animated music video — the kind with cinematic visual storytelling, professional rendering, and the production quality this track deserved — runs $500,000 or more at the high end. Even a respectable indie production would have been tens of thousands of dollars I don't have and wouldn't spend even if I did.

I wasn't a potential customer for those services. I was priced out of that market before I even walked in the door.

So AI didn't take a job from a production company. There was no job to take. What AI *did* do was create a category of client that didn't exist before: the independent artist who can now produce at a quality level that was previously only accessible to people with label money behind them.

That's not displacement. That's market *expansion*.

This is the argument nobody in the "AI is stealing jobs" conversation wants to have, because it's more complicated than the bumper sticker version. The production companies worth worrying about are the ones serving major labels who were already going to use AI anyway. The indie artist with a Giger vision and a VEGAS Pro license? We were never in the same economic conversation to begin with.

## What This Actually Means for Independent Artists

I spent twenty-plus years building an audience the hard way. Half-empty bars in Cincinnati. Local support slots at Riverbend on the same nights as KISS, Mötley Crüe, Megadeth. One ruptured eardrum freshman year that ended my trumpet career and redirected me toward a guitar that I've been playing ever since. A PhD in Marketing Management that I earned while still gigging. A TikTok channel that went from 2,000 to 15,000 followers in under twelve months because a friend told me to stop making excuses and get on the platform.

The throughline in all of it is this: the tools keep changing, and the artists who survive are the ones who pick them up instead of arguing about whether they should exist.

AI video production is a tool. It is a genuinely powerful one. And for independent musicians, it closes a gap that used to be unbridgeable — the gap between having a great recording and being able to *present* it with the visual weight it deserves.

Ozzy didn't worry about whether the synthesizer was going to replace the guitar player. He just kept making music that mattered.

That's the move.

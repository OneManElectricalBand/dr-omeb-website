Marketing & Attention

Nobody Owes You Attention

What a bad LinkedIn exchange taught me about attention debt, counterfeit engagement, and the difference between marketing and keeping score.

By Dr. Michael Carr · Dr. OMEBOriginally published July 10, 2026Revised September 4, 20267 min read

Citizens of Rock n' Roll,

A man once asked me for marketing advice. Years later, after reposting some of my content, he informed me that I had failed to repay him with enough attention of my own. I gave him the honest professional answer he had originally requested.

He blocked me.

I am not naming him, his organization, or the argument. The lesson is more useful than the feud, and the details would only invite people to choose sides when the real problem is something almost everyone trying to grow an audience eventually encounters.

He treated attention as a debt he could invoice.

Attention is not a loyalty card

The implied contract was simple: I reposted you, therefore you owe me a repost. On paper, that can look like mutual support. In practice, it often becomes a barter market where everyone trades proof of activity and nobody receives meaningful attention.

I call it counterfeit attention.

Counterfeit attention looks good in screenshots. Accounts dutifully reshare one another. Numbers move. Everybody can point to evidence that they “supported” the group. But if the people doing the sharing did not watch, listen, care, attend, subscribe, or buy, the engagement is an empty receipt.

Demanded attention is worth exactly zero, because valuable attention only exists when a person gives it freely.

Everything scales except the human day

Distribution can scale. The same post can reach forty people or four million without requiring four million times more labor. A content library can scale. A follower count can scale. A mailing list can scale.

The number of waking hours available to the audience does not.

I have the same number of hours now that I had when almost nobody was watching. The audience does too. Every creator, business, news outlet, streaming service, friend, employer, and family member is competing for pieces of the same finite human day.

That scarcity makes attention valuable. It also makes people feel entitled to it when they believe they have already paid.

The market does not owe you for your history

The advice I gave was uncomfortable: a long-running project without a native discovery engine cannot solve its audience problem by demanding more favors from existing connections. It has to become easier to discover, easier to understand, and more compelling to join.

That diagnosis applies well beyond one conversation. Artists assume people owe a listen because the song took months to make. Businesses assume customers owe patience because the company has been around for decades. Experts assume credentials should purchase interest before they have explained why the subject matters.

The audience sees none of the sunk cost. It sees only the next choice on the screen.

I have been the guy keeping score

This is not a story about my superior enlightenment. In 2012, the recession cut my guitar studio from roughly fifty-five students to ten. I spent longer than I would like to admit being angry at a market that owed me nothing.

Years later, when my friend Alexa told me to try TikTok, I defended the way I had always performed. I had spent two decades playing bars and traditional gigs, so that investment began doing my thinking for me. I assumed the audience for what I did had disappeared when, in reality, I had stopped looking for the room where it was gathering.

The difference was not that I enjoyed the diagnosis. The difference was that I eventually tested it. That experiment became Rock n' Roll Church and an entirely new way of thinking about live music.

Stop asking what they owe you

Before the next pitch, post, direct message, press release, or request for support, skip the question of what the other person owes you. Ask what you are giving them.

  • Is the value obvious before the context?
  • Does the message respect the person’s limited time?
  • Would someone care without a private obligation to you?
  • Are you creating genuine participation—or merely trading activity?

Following someone is not a down payment on a future favor. Reposting someone is not a claim against their calendar. Landing in an inbox does not create a duty to respond.

Attention is earned, never owed. The moment you catch yourself keeping score, you have stopped marketing and started collecting.

Your Brand Is Losing a War for Attention: KISS Figured Out How to Win It 50 Years Ago

Most artists think they compete with similar artists. KISS understood that the real competition is every other way the audience could spend its time.

By Dr. Michael Carr · Dr. OMEBOriginally published May 7, 2026Revised September 4, 20268 min read

Citizens of Rock n' Roll,

Most bands think their competition is another band in the same genre. Most businesses think their competition is the company selling a similar product. That is usually too narrow.

Your competition is every other way the audience can spend the next ten minutes.

KISS understood that before the rest of the music business had language for the attention economy. They were not merely trying to write better songs than another rock band. They were competing with movies, sports, television, comic books, toys, spectacle, gossip, and the very human desire to feel part of something larger than ordinary life.

They built accordingly.

They made the promise obvious

You did not need a paragraph to understand what a KISS ticket promised. The makeup, costumes, logo, fire, blood, platform boots, characters, and scale communicated before the first chord.

That is not superficial decoration. It is positioning made visible.

A strong brand helps the audience answer three questions quickly:

  1. What is this?
  2. Why is it different?
  3. What will I feel if I enter the world?

KISS did not ask people to study the band long enough to discover the difference. They put the difference on the poster.

They treated the show as the product

The recording mattered, but the product was larger than the recording. The show created stories people could repeat afterward. The visual identity made a distant arena seat feel like part of the event. The members functioned as recognizable characters. Merchandise allowed the audience to carry the world home.

The lesson is not that every independent artist needs fire-breathing equipment and a licensing department. The lesson is that the experience surrounding the music can make the music easier to remember.

If the song is the only distinctive part of the offer, every other touchpoint is asking the audience to do extra work.

They gave fans an identity

The KISS Army is one of the clearest examples of community language in rock. It turns passive listeners into members. It gives the audience a name for itself, a way to recognize one another, and a reason to display affiliation.

That does not mean slapping a nickname on followers automatically creates a community. The language works only when recurring experiences, symbols, participation, and relationships give it substance.

That lesson directly influenced how I think about Rock n' Roll Church. The Congregation is not a cute label for a mailing list. It is the name for the people who return, request songs, talk to one another, share the performances, and help keep the room alive.

One night at Riverbend

I have opened the pre-show plaza outside Riverbend Music Center in Cincinnati many times. It is a useful place to watch major touring operations from close range.

On July 15, 2014, KISS and Def Leppard were playing the main stage. Before my set, I noticed a credentialed photographer moving through the area, taking pictures and making calls. After I came off stage, he found me and introduced himself as Gene Simmons’s guitar technician.

He told me Gene had checked out the act for a few minutes, liked what he saw, and asked him to give me some picks.

I still have them.

The obvious value of that memory is personal. The strategic value is what the story suggests about attention. Someone inside an enormous touring machine was still curious about what was happening on the edge of the venue. Great brands do not become great by assuming the environment is beneath their notice.

The KISS test for a modern brand

You do not need to copy KISS. In fact, copying the surface without understanding the strategy creates a tribute act.

Use the underlying questions:

DistinctiveCould someone recognize the brand without seeing the name?
ExperientialDoes the audience have something to talk about after the transaction?
ParticipatoryCan people join, return, request, collect, or contribute?
ConsistentDo the music, visuals, language, merch, and live experience tell the same story?
ExpandableCan one idea become content, live moments, products, press, and community?
MemorableWhat survives after the audience scrolls away?

Make them remember you were there

The point is not to be louder in every possible way. Noise without identity is still noise. The point is to become unmistakable.

KISS aligned product, promotion, performance, personality, community, and merchandise around one clear promise: this would not feel like an ordinary rock show.

Independent artists do not have their budget. We do have the ability to make deliberate choices. A recognizable pair of blue glasses. A one-man-band premise people can explain in one sentence. A Sunday gathering called Rock n' Roll Church. A congregation that knows it belongs in the room.

Your brand is not what you repeatedly claim. It is what the audience can repeatedly recognize.

Do not ask only how to make the next post louder. Ask how to make the entire experience impossible to confuse with somebody else’s.

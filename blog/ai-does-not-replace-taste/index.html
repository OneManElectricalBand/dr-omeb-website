AI Does Not Replace Taste: Why Creative Experience Matters More Now

The same tool in two different hands does not produce the same work. AI increases leverage; it does not erase the value of knowing what good looks like.

By Dr. Michael Carr · Dr. OMEBOriginally published April 30, 2026Revised September 4, 20269 min read

Citizens of Rock n' Roll,

The argument about AI often begins with the wrong question: Can the machine make something?

Of course it can. The more useful question is whether the person directing it knows what the finished work is supposed to accomplish.

Put an experienced designer and a novice behind the same image model. Give them the same brief. They will not produce the same result. The experienced designer understands composition, hierarchy, contrast, brand language, and when the first answer is merely competent. The novice may produce something faster, but speed does not automatically create judgment.

AI can move the starting line. It does not tell you where the finish line belongs.

Experience is the part that does not fit inside the prompt

A video editor still needs to feel when a cut arrives too early. A copywriter still needs to recognize when a headline is technically clear and emotionally dead. A musician still needs to hear whether a performance breathes, drags, swings, or sounds like every preset in the folder.

The model can generate possibilities. The human has to select, reject, revise, combine, verify, and accept responsibility for the result.

That is why AI frequently produces what people now call “slop”: content that is polished enough to exist and generic enough to disappear. The problem is not merely that a machine participated. The problem is that nobody with taste stopped the process and said, “No. That is not good enough.”

The concerns are real

Creative people are not irrational for worrying about AI. Training data, consent, compensation, copyright, job displacement, homogenized aesthetics, deceptive synthetic media, and the temptation to replace craft with volume are legitimate issues.

Using the tools responsibly requires more than cheering for disruption. It requires transparency about how work was made, respect for the rights of other creators, human review, source verification, and a willingness not to automate something simply because it can be automated.

I do not believe the correct response is denial. I also do not believe every criticism is fear from someone who “doesn’t get it.” Both positions are lazy.

What AI changed for me

My brain has always produced ideas faster than I can execute them. I am a musician, marketer, educator, writer, and a major operator inside my own business. Before AI, the distance between an idea and a usable draft often killed the idea.

AI shortened that distance. It helps me organize research questions, test structures, explore visual directions, draft production plans, and identify options I can then evaluate. It functions like leverage for a one-person operation.

That does not mean every output is usable. A large percentage is not. The work often lies in recognizing exactly what is wrong: the face drifted, the guitar is impossible, the copy became generic, the source does not support the claim, or the tone sounds like an intern swallowed a branding textbook.

That last one happens a lot.

The weird career became an advantage

I spent years feeling like the wrong person in the wrong room—too rock n' roll for one environment, too academic for another. I was advised to cut my hair if I wanted business people to take me seriously. I did not.

Eventually I stopped trying to make every part of my background look conventional and started using the combination.

At a small independent grocery store in Rivertown, Ohio, that meant creating odd, memorable campaigns—Pink Floyd references, “Boneless Bananas,” and ideas a larger operation might have sanded smooth before anyone saw them. In music, it meant building an act where one person records the band and then performs the lead guitar and vocals live. On TikTok, it meant discovering that classic rock, marketing instincts, and a weekly community could become Rock n' Roll Church.

The common advantage was never access to a particular tool. It was a point of view developed across several kinds of work.

AI raises the value of direction

When execution becomes cheaper, direction becomes more important. More people can make a passable image, video, website, or campaign. That expands opportunity—and floods the market with average work.

The differentiators become:

  • knowing which problem is worth solving;
  • recognizing a distinctive idea before it is normalized;
  • understanding the audience well enough to reject the wrong answer;
  • combining technical output with lived experience;
  • checking the facts and owning the consequences;
  • and knowing when the machine should leave the room.

That is not a guarantee that every creative career becomes safer. It is an argument that expertise still matters after the software becomes easier.

Use the machine without surrendering the work

My rule is simple: AI can assist the process, but it does not get final authority over identity, truth, or taste.

I disclose meaningful AI use. I do not present generated evidence as a real photograph. I verify factual claims. I keep the human performance at the center of the music. And I remain willing to throw away an expensive generation when it does not look like me, sound like me, or serve the idea.

The artists who benefit most will not be the people who press a button fastest. They will be the people who bring something the button does not contain.

AI does not replace taste. It reveals who brought some.
